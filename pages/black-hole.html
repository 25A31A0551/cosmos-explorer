Black Holes

Regions of spacetime where gravity is so strong that beyond the event horizon, nothing can escape to distant observers, including light.

A Cosmic Gravity Trap

A black hole can form when a sufficiently massive object collapses under its own gravity. The resulting concentration of mass strongly curves spacetime.

The boundary surrounding a black hole is called the event horizon. Once an object crosses this boundary, it cannot return to the outside universe through ordinary escape.

What Is Inside?

General relativity predicts that the collapse of matter can lead toward a region called a singularity, where the mathematical description of spacetime becomes extreme.

However, physicists do not yet have a complete theory of quantum gravity. Therefore, the ultimate physical nature of the interior of a black hole remains an open research question.

Accretion Disks

Matter falling toward a black hole can form a rapidly rotating accretion disk. Friction and extreme gravitational effects can heat this material to enormous temperatures, producing powerful radiation.

Some black holes also produce relativistic jets that can extend enormous distances into surrounding space.

A black hole can begin its story with the death of a massive star. When a sufficiently massive star exhausts the nuclear fuel supporting its core, gravity can overwhelm the internal pressure that previously held the star apart. The core collapses dramatically, and depending on the mass and conditions, the final result can be a black hole. The surrounding material may be blasted outward in a supernova while the collapsed core becomes an object whose gravitational field is so strong that an event horizon forms. The event horizon is not a physical surface but a boundary beyond which signals cannot escape to distant observers.

Near a black hole, space and time behave in ways that challenge ordinary intuition. Matter falling toward the black hole can form a rapidly rotating accretion disk, becoming extremely hot and producing intense radiation before crossing the event horizon. Some black holes launch powerful jets of particles from their surrounding environments, extending enormous distances into space. Massive black holes also exist at the centers of galaxies, including the supermassive black hole at the center of the Milky Way. Their growth is connected with the evolution of galaxies, although many details of that relationship remain under investigation.

The darkest mystery of black holes begins at the deepest theoretical level. General relativity predicts a singularity under certain idealized conditions, but physicists expect quantum gravity to become important before such a description can be considered complete. Black holes also create the famous information problem, asking how quantum information behaves when matter falls beyond an event horizon. Hawking radiation suggests that black holes can slowly lose energy and potentially evaporate over extraordinary timescales. Modern observations using gravitational waves, radio telescopes, and other instruments have transformed black holes from mathematical predictions into directly studied astronomical objects while leaving their deepest secrets unresolved.

Black Hole Types

Type Description
Stellar-Mass Black Hole Typically forms from the collapse of a massive star or through related stellar processes.
Intermediate-Mass Black Hole A proposed population between stellar-mass and supermassive black holes.
Supermassive Black Hole Contains millions to billions of solar masses and commonly exists at galaxy centers.
Primordial Black Hole Hypothetical black holes that may have formed from extreme density fluctuations in the early Universe.

Can a Black Hole Destroy the Universe?

No known physical process suggests that a typical black hole can simply consume the entire Universe. Black holes influence objects within their gravitational environment, while the Universe continues to expand on the largest scales.
