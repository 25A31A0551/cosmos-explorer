Mercury (&9791;)

The innermost planet in the Solar System and the smallest of the eight major planets.

Geological & Physical Characteristics

Mercury is a rocky terrestrial body with a heavily cratered surface resembling Earth's Moon. It has a massive metallic core that accounts for roughly 85% of its radius, leading to a unusually high planetary density of 5.427 g/cm3.

Mercury exhibits a unique 3:2 spin-orbit resonance, completing three rotations on its axis for every two orbits around the Sun. Because it lacks a substantial atmosphere to distribute thermal energy, surface temperatures fluctuate wildly between extreme highs of 430°C on the sunlit side and -180°C on the night side.

Mercury's story begins in the violent construction zone of the young Solar System, when countless rocky bodies collided, merged, and were sometimes destroyed. Mercury formed approximately 4.5 billion years ago close to the newborn Sun, where intense heat and powerful solar radiation influenced the material available to build it. One unusual result was a planet with an enormous metallic core compared with its rocky exterior. Scientists continue to investigate why Mercury contains such a large iron-rich interior. One possibility involves extremely energetic impacts early in its history that stripped away some of its outer rocky material, while other models suggest that its unusual composition could have developed naturally in the inner Solar System.

Mercury looks like a frozen piece of ancient history covered with craters, cliffs, and enormous impact basins. Without a substantial atmosphere to distribute heat, the surface experiences extreme temperature differences. Daytime temperatures can rise above 400 degrees Celsius, while nighttime temperatures can fall below minus 170 degrees Celsius. Surprisingly, permanently shadowed craters near Mercury's poles can contain water ice despite the planet being so close to the Sun. These dark polar regions never receive direct sunlight, allowing ancient deposits of ice to survive inside deep craters. Mercury also has a very unusual relationship between its rotation and orbit, completing three rotations for every two trips around the Sun.

The dangerous side of Mercury is its proximity to the Sun. Solar radiation is intense, the surface is continuously exposed to space, and the planet has no thick atmosphere to protect it from meteoroid impacts or extreme thermal conditions. Yet Mercury possesses a global magnetic field, something unexpected for such a small planet. Spacecraft observations have revealed a world far more complicated than the cratered sphere first imagined by astronomers. Missions such as Mariner 10 and MESSENGER transformed our understanding of Mercury, while newer exploration continues to investigate its core, magnetic field, geological history, and polar ice. Mercury remains a small planet carrying an enormous record of Solar System history.
