Earth (♁)

The third planet from the Sun and the only known astronomical body supporting biological life.

Geosphere & Atmosphere Systems

Earth is the largest and most massive terrestrial planet, featuring liquid water oceans covering approximately 70.8% of its surface. Its interior is differentiated into a solid iron-nickel inner core, liquid outer core, mantle, and crust.

The movement of material within Earth's liquid outer core generates its global geomagnetic field. Earth's atmosphere consists primarily of nitrogen and oxygen, with smaller amounts of other gases. Active plate tectonics recycle materials and influence Earth's surface and climate over geological timescales.

Earth formed approximately 4.5 billion years ago from the same rotating disk of material that surrounded the young Sun. Dust grains collided and stuck together, gradually becoming larger rocks, planetesimals, and eventually planetary embryos. Repeated impacts shaped the young Earth, and one of the most important collisions may have involved a Mars-sized body commonly called Theia. The impact is thought to have produced debris that eventually gathered into the Moon. Early Earth was extremely hot, volcanic, and repeatedly bombarded by asteroids, but over immense periods of time its surface cooled, oceans formed, and a complex atmosphere developed. The planet slowly transformed from a violent young world into the blue world seen from space today.

Earth's environment is unusually diverse. Liquid oceans cover most of its surface, while mountains, deserts, forests, glaciers, grasslands, and deep oceans create countless habitats. The atmosphere is dominated by nitrogen and oxygen and contains smaller quantities of water vapor, carbon dioxide, and other gases. Earth's magnetic field protects the surface from much of the charged particle radiation carried by the solar wind. The planet's active geological engine continuously recycles crust through plate tectonics, creating mountains, earthquakes, volcanoes, and ocean basins. The combination of liquid water, chemical elements, energy sources, a protective atmosphere, and long-term environmental stability created conditions in which life could emerge and diversify.

Earth also has a darker and more fragile side. Asteroid impacts, supervolcanic eruptions, climate shifts, mass extinctions, radiation events, and changes in atmospheric chemistry have repeatedly threatened life throughout geological history. Yet life has survived extraordinary environmental transformations. The fossil record shows that today's ecosystems are only one chapter in a much longer story. Human civilization has become another powerful geological influence by changing landscapes, atmospheric composition, oceans, and ecosystems. Earth remains the only known world with life, making it not simply another planet but the reference point against which scientists compare every potentially habitable world discovered beyond the Solar System.
