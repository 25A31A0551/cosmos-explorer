Venus (♀)

The second planet from the Sun, often styled as Earth's twin in size but vastly different in climate.

Atmospheric Physics & Surface Conditions

Venus possesses an extremely dense atmosphere composed of approximately 96.5% carbon dioxide and 3.5% nitrogen, generating a powerful global greenhouse effect.

The surface pressure is about 92 times Earth's sea-level pressure. The planet is permanently surrounded by thick clouds containing sulfuric acid. Venus rotates very slowly in a retrograde direction, taking about 243 Earth days to complete one rotation, while its orbital period is about 225 Earth days.

Venus began its existence from the same ancient disk of gas and dust that produced Earth and the other rocky planets. As material accumulated close to the Sun, countless collisions gradually built a young planetary body. Early Venus may have possessed conditions very different from the hostile world visible today. Its geological history is still debated because the planet's surface is relatively young compared with heavily cratered worlds such as Mercury. Massive volcanic activity appears to have played a major role in reshaping the planet. The thick atmosphere gradually became dominated by carbon dioxide, while clouds of sulfuric acid developed high above the surface, creating one of the most extreme environments known among the planets.

Walking across Venus in imagination would be almost impossible. The surface temperature is around 465 degrees Celsius, hot enough to melt lead, and the atmospheric pressure is roughly ninety times that of Earth's surface. Sunlight reaching the ground would be filtered through thick clouds, producing a dim, orange-yellow environment. Venus rotates extremely slowly and in the opposite direction to most planets, meaning the Sun would appear to rise in the west and set in the east. Beneath the clouds are mountains, volcanic plains, enormous highland regions, and structures created by geological forces. Scientists have found evidence suggesting that volcanic activity may still occur today, making Venus potentially more geologically active than its calm appearance suggests.

The darkest lesson of Venus is its extreme greenhouse atmosphere. Carbon dioxide traps heat so efficiently that the planet became far hotter than Mercury despite receiving less solar energy. Venus demonstrates how atmospheric composition can completely transform a planetary climate. Its thick clouds also hide the surface from ordinary telescopes, forcing scientists to use radar and spacecraft instruments to map the terrain. Soviet Venera missions successfully reached the surface and transmitted photographs before the environment destroyed them. Modern missions continue to treat Venus as a natural laboratory for understanding climate evolution, volcanic worlds, atmospheric chemistry, and the possible futures of rocky planets.
