Theia

The ancient planetary body believed to have collided with the young Earth and contributed to the formation of the Moon.

The Planet That Changed Earth Forever

Approximately 4.5 billion years ago, the Solar System was still forming. Young planets and planetary embryos moved through unstable orbits, making enormous collisions possible.

One of these ancient planetary bodies may have been Theia. Scientists use this name for the hypothetical object involved in the giant-impact model of Moon formation.

Theia is not a planet that exists today. Its existence is inferred from models of planetary formation, the Earth-Moon system, and the physical evidence preserved on Earth and the Moon.

Theia is the name commonly given to a hypothesized planetary body involved in the giant-impact model for the formation of the Moon. Early in Earth's history, the Solar System was crowded with growing planetary embryos moving through unstable orbits. Theia may have been roughly comparable in size to Mars and could have formed in a region of the inner Solar System before its orbit eventually became unstable. At some point, gravitational interactions may have placed Theia on a collision course with the young Earth. The impact would have been unimaginably energetic, transforming both bodies and throwing enormous amounts of molten and vaporized material into orbit around Earth.

Over time, the debris from this collision could have gathered under gravity and formed the Moon. The giant-impact model helps explain several characteristics of the Earth-Moon system, including the Moon's relatively large size compared with Earth and aspects of its composition and orbital behavior. However, the exact details of the impact remain an active area of research. Scientists investigate whether the collision was direct or angled, how much material came from Earth versus Theia, and how the resulting debris evolved. Computer simulations allow researchers to recreate possible collisions and compare their outcomes with geological and geochemical evidence preserved on Earth and the Moon.

The mysterious side of Theia is that no intact object bearing that name has been discovered. Theia is a scientific reconstruction of a possible ancient planetary body, and its exact nature remains uncertain. Some research has explored whether unusual chemical signatures deep inside Earth could preserve remnants of the ancient collision, although interpretations continue to be debated. If the giant impact occurred, it would mean that an enormous planetary disaster helped create one of the most important companions in human history. The Moon later influenced Earth's tides, rotation, night environment, and cultural development, making the hypothetical story of Theia part of the much larger story of how Earth became the world we know today.

The Giant Impact

The leading giant-impact hypothesis proposes that Theia collided with the young proto-Earth approximately 4.5 billion years ago.

The collision would have released an enormous amount of energy. Rock from the two bodies could have been melted, vaporized, and thrown into orbit around the young Earth.

Over time, this orbiting material could have collided and gathered together under gravity, eventually forming the Moon.

How Could the Moon Have Formed?

The giant-impact model explains several important properties of the Earth-Moon system. A large collision can produce a disk of debris around Earth, from which a satellite can form.

The exact details of the collision are still being investigated. Scientists use computer simulations to test different impact speeds, angles, masses, and compositions.

Different models can produce different amounts of Earth and impactor material in the resulting Moon.

Did Theia Become Part of Earth?

One of the most fascinating possibilities is that Theia did not completely disappear.

Earth's deep mantle contains two enormous regions known as Large Low-Velocity Provinces, or LLVPs. They are located deep inside Earth near the core-mantle boundary.

A 2023 scientific study proposed that these unusual structures could contain dense remnants of Theia's mantle that became trapped inside Earth during the giant impact.

Computer simulations suggest that some Theia material could have survived the collision, sunk through the Earth's mantle, and eventually accumulated deep inside the planet.

The Japanese Scientist and the Theia Hypothesis

Japanese planetary scientist Yoshinori Miyazaki was one of the researchers involved in the 2023 study investigating whether remnants of Theia could explain the large structures deep inside Earth.

The study used computer modelling to investigate how material from the impactor could behave after the Moon-forming collision.

The research does not prove that the LLVPs are definitely pieces of Theia. Instead, it provides a possible explanation that can be tested against geological and geophysical observations.

A Planet That Disappeared

Imagine Earth approximately 4.5 billion years ago.

There was no Moon in the sky as we see it today. Earth was young, hot, and still surrounded by the debris left over from planetary formation.

Then another planetary body approached.

Theia collided with Earth.

The impact may have destroyed both worlds as separate planetary bodies. But their material did not simply vanish.

Some of that material may have formed the Moon.

Some became part of Earth.

And according to one scientific model, some of Theia may still be hidden deep beneath Earth's surface.

The Mystery Beneath Our Feet

If the Large Low-Velocity Provinces really contain ancient Theia material, Earth could preserve a physical remnant of another planetary world inside its own interior.

That possibility would mean that the remains of a planet involved in the formation of the Moon may still exist thousands of kilometres below the surface.

However, scientists are still investigating this idea. The LLVPs could have other origins, and additional evidence is needed to determine their exact composition and history.

Theia, Earth and the Moon

The giant-impact hypothesis connects three worlds into one extraordinary story: the ancient Theia, the young Earth, and the Moon.

Theia may have collided with Earth, creating an enormous debris field. That debris eventually became the Moon, while some of the impactor's material may have become permanently incorporated into Earth.

The Moon in the night sky could therefore be one of the most visible clues to an ancient planetary collision.

And somewhere deep inside Earth, the final remnants of that lost world may still be waiting to be understood.
