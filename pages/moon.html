Moon (☾)

Earth's only natural satellite and the fifth-largest natural satellite in the Solar System.

Earth's Moon

The Moon is Earth's only natural satellite and has played an important role in Earth's history. It is approximately 4.51 billion years old and formed early in the history of the Solar System.

The Moon has no substantial atmosphere like Earth. Its surface is covered by craters, mountains, valleys, and large dark plains called maria. These features were produced by impacts, volcanic activity, and geological processes over billions of years.

The Moon's gravity is about one-sixth of Earth's gravity. Because of this, an object weighs much less on the Moon than it does on Earth.

The Moon's story is connected directly to the violent childhood of Earth. The leading giant-impact model proposes that a large planetary body, commonly called Theia, collided with the young Earth more than four billion years ago. A cloud of hot debris was thrown into orbit, gradually gathering together under gravity until the Moon formed. The newborn Moon was probably covered by a global ocean of molten rock, and over time its surface cooled to form a crust. Ancient volcanic activity later produced large dark plains known as maria. These regions preserve evidence of impacts and geological processes that occurred billions of years ago, making the Moon a natural archive of the early Solar System.

The Moon has no thick atmosphere and therefore experiences an extreme environment. Temperatures can change dramatically between sunlight and darkness, while the surface is constantly exposed to cosmic radiation and microscopic impacts. Its famous craters were produced by collisions with asteroids and comets, many of which happened billions of years ago. Near the lunar poles, some deep craters never receive direct sunlight, allowing water ice to survive in permanent shadow. The Moon's surface also contains a fine layer of dust called regolith that was produced by countless impacts. Because there is no substantial atmosphere to erase footprints, disturbances on the surface can remain for extremely long periods.

The Moon's influence on Earth is enormous despite its relatively small size. Its gravity produces ocean tides and contributes to the long-term stability of Earth's rotational axis. The Moon is slowly moving farther away from Earth, while Earth's rotation gradually changes because of tidal interactions. Humans have visited the lunar surface through the Apollo missions, collecting rocks that revealed important information about lunar formation and history. Robotic missions have since mapped the Moon in extraordinary detail. Future exploration focuses strongly on the polar regions, where water ice could become an important resource for scientific research and potentially future human exploration.

Moon's Orbit and Rotation

The Moon orbits Earth at an average distance of approximately 384,400 kilometers. It takes about 27.3 days to complete one orbit relative to the distant stars.

The Moon is tidally locked to Earth. This means that the Moon rotates once on its axis in approximately the same amount of time that it takes to orbit Earth. As a result, the same general side of the Moon always faces Earth.

The Moon's changing appearance from Earth is called its phases. The major phases include new moon, first quarter, full moon, and third quarter.

Moon Surface

The lunar surface contains millions of impact craters formed by collisions with asteroids and other objects. Some of the largest impact structures are enormous basins that were later partly filled by ancient volcanic lava.

The dark regions visible from Earth are called lunar maria. They are broad plains of basaltic rock formed by ancient volcanic activity. The brighter regions are generally older, heavily cratered highlands.

Why the Moon Matters to Earth

The Moon has a major influence on Earth's oceans through its gravitational interaction with Earth. This interaction produces most of the regular ocean tides.

The Moon also helps scientists understand the early history of the Solar System. Because its surface has experienced relatively little erosion compared with Earth, many ancient geological features remain preserved.

Moon Exploration

The Moon has been explored by robotic spacecraft and human missions. The Apollo missions of NASA carried astronauts to the lunar surface between 1969 and 1972, and astronauts collected lunar rocks and soil for scientific study.

Modern lunar missions continue to study the Moon's surface, interior, resources, and permanently shadowed regions near its polar areas.
