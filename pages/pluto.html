Pluto

A distant icy dwarf planet located in the Kuiper Belt beyond the orbit of Neptune.

A World Beyond Neptune

Pluto is one of the best-known objects in the outer Solar System. It lies in the Kuiper Belt, a region populated by many icy bodies beyond Neptune.

Pluto was discovered in 1930 and was classified as the ninth planet for decades. In 2006, the International Astronomical Union established a formal definition of planet that placed Pluto in the dwarf planet category.

Pluto formed in the distant Kuiper Belt, far beyond the orbit of Neptune, where temperatures were cold enough for water ice and other frozen compounds to survive for billions of years. It is a dwarf planet rather than one of the eight major planets, but its geological complexity surprised scientists when the New Horizons spacecraft reached it in 2015. Pluto's surface contains mountains, glaciers, valleys, plains, and enormous regions covered by frozen nitrogen. Its famous heart-shaped Tombaugh Regio became one of the most recognizable landscapes discovered in the outer Solar System. Pluto's history shows that even small and distant worlds can remain geologically interesting long after their formation.

Pluto experiences an extreme climate controlled by its highly elliptical and tilted orbit. During portions of its long journey around the Sun, frozen nitrogen and other materials can move between the surface and atmosphere. When sunlight becomes stronger, surface ices can sublimate into gas, creating a temporary atmosphere. As Pluto moves farther away, those gases can freeze back onto the surface. The dwarf planet has a thin atmosphere containing nitrogen and smaller quantities of methane and carbon monoxide. Beneath the surface, scientists continue investigating whether enough internal heat could once have allowed a subsurface ocean to exist.

The dark and mysterious side of Pluto comes from its enormous distance, weak sunlight, and difficult observational conditions. A signal from Earth requires hours to travel to and from the Pluto system, making communication with spacecraft challenging. New Horizons revealed mountains made largely from water ice, surprisingly young terrain, atmospheric haze layers, and evidence of geological activity. Pluto also has several moons, with Charon being so large relative to Pluto that the two objects are sometimes described as a binary system. Pluto changed from a tiny point of light into a complex world, reminding scientists that the outer Solar System contains many more geological stories waiting to be discovered.

Pluto's Surface

Pluto has a complex surface containing mountains, plains, glaciers, valleys, and impact craters. Its surface includes nitrogen, methane, and carbon monoxide ices.

One of its most recognizable features is a large bright region informally known as Sputnik Planitia, a vast basin dominated by nitrogen ice.

Pluto's Atmosphere

Pluto has a thin atmosphere primarily containing nitrogen, with smaller quantities of methane and carbon monoxide. The atmosphere changes as Pluto moves along its highly elongated orbit.

Pluto Metrics

Metric Data
Classification Dwarf Planet
Average Distance from Sun Approximately 5.9 billion km
Mean Radius Approximately 1,188 km
Mass Approximately 1.31 × 1022 kg
Orbital Period Approximately 248 Earth Years
Rotation Period Approximately 6.4 Earth Days
Largest Moon Charon
Known Moons Five known moons

The New Horizons Mission

NASA's New Horizons spacecraft performed the first close flyby of Pluto in July 2015. The mission revealed a surprisingly diverse world with mountains, glaciers, atmospheric haze, and complex geological structures.
