The Solar System

Our planetary system formed approximately 4.6 billion years ago from the gravitational collapse of a giant interstellar molecular cloud.

The Sun

Star | Diameter: 1,392,700 km | Core Temp: 15,000,000°C

The story of the Sun begins long before Earth had oceans, mountains, or even a name. About 4.6 billion years ago, a vast cloud of gas and dust collapsed under its own gravity, slowly concentrating material toward its center. As the cloud contracted, the central region became hotter and denser until the pressure and temperature became powerful enough for nuclear fusion to begin. Hydrogen nuclei started combining to form helium, releasing enormous amounts of energy. That ancient ignition transformed a cold cloud of interstellar material into the star that would eventually control the architecture of our entire planetary system. The young Sun was more active and violent than the calm golden disk we see today, surrounded by a turbulent disk of leftover material from which planets, moons, asteroids, and comets eventually formed.

From Earth, the Sun can appear peaceful, but its surface is a constantly changing ocean of plasma. Enormous magnetic fields twist through its atmosphere, producing sunspots, prominences, solar flares, and coronal mass ejections. The temperature of the visible surface is around 5,500 degrees Celsius, while the corona above it can reach millions of degrees. This strange temperature relationship remains one of the important questions of solar physics. The Sun's solar wind continuously flows outward through the Solar System, creating a huge bubble called the heliosphere. When powerful solar storms reach Earth, they can disturb satellites, radio communication, navigation systems, and electrical infrastructure while also creating spectacular auroras near the poles.

The darker side of the Sun is not darkness in the ordinary sense but the enormous danger contained within its energy. A sufficiently powerful solar eruption can release billions of tons of charged particles into space, and ancient evidence suggests that the young Sun experienced much stronger activity than today. Yet the same star that can produce destructive space weather also makes life on Earth possible. Its energy drives Earth's climate, powers photosynthesis, shapes atmospheric circulation, and provides nearly all of the energy supporting the biosphere. The Sun will not shine forever. In roughly five billion years it is expected to exhaust much of its core hydrogen, expand into a red giant, and dramatically alter the inner Solar System before eventually leaving behind a dense white dwarf.
